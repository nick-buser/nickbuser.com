The lab's one GPU, a 16 GB RTX 5060 Ti, is shared by four always-on models and a
queue of experiments. A Qwen2.5-7B served by vLLM used to be started by hand and
left running, holding about 12 GB of the card. I wanted three things: keep the
card free for experiments, let any app's request wake the model, and make that
wake as short as possible. Fifteen measured steps got there.

## How it was measured

Engine changes ran first on a **twin**: a disposable copy of the production server
with the same model, flags and memory claim, launched as an Argo workflow, so
experiments never touched the live model. A harness read each run's vLLM log and
pod events and split the start into named phases, and wrote a ledger row before
anyone interpreted it. Most arms ran two or three times on the same card. The
adoption rule was set before the first measurement: keep a change if it cuts the
cold start by at least 20% and costs at most 5% of batch-1 decode speed.
Anything outside that was a judgment call. Midway through, the deciding number
changed from the model's time to Ready to a real client's wait for its first
token.

## The engine: 141 seconds to 40

</Figure>

The baseline took 141 seconds to Ready, and 104 of them were engine work around
CUDA graphs: 52 seconds profiling their memory and 52 capturing them
([step 2](/experiments/llm-cold-start/02-build-the-measurement-rig)). Pinning the
KV cache size skipped the profiling pass but saved only ten seconds, because the
pass had been paying first-use kernel costs that the capture then paid instead
([3](/experiments/llm-cold-start/03-skip-the-memory-profiling-pass)). Capturing
fewer graphs, or none, cut the start by up to 43%, and appeared to wreck batch-1
decode ([4](/experiments/llm-cold-start/04-capture-fewer-cuda-graphs)).

A warm-up request before the decode benchmark showed steady decode within 2% in
every arm. The graph-less arms had only moved about 40 seconds from startup to
their first request, so a caller's wake-to-answer improved by 7%, not 38%
([5](/experiments/llm-cold-start/05-measure-decode-fairly)). That changed the
yardstick for everything after it. Turn on the first answer in Fig. 2 and the
graph-less arms' lead mostly disappears.

The 40 seconds looked like framework kernel compilation repeated on every pod
start. Keeping Triton's and FlashInfer's caches on a persistent volume changed
nothing ([6](/experiments/llm-cold-start/06-persist-the-kernel-caches)), but the
engine log showed it idle for 40 seconds before answering in 5, and a
neighbouring pod held a cache from the CUDA driver itself. On this GPU
generation the driver compiles part of the image's kernels from PTX on first use
and caches the result in the container's home directory, which disappeared with
every pod. Keeping that 69 MB cache on the volume cut the start from 80 seconds
to 40 without graphs, and the first request from 44.7 seconds to 5.1
([7](/experiments/llm-cold-start/07-persist-the-driver-cache)). It was the
largest single gain, and its explanation is an inference that fits every
observation without a driver-level trace to confirm it.

Production took the driver cache, the pinned KV cache and no CUDA graphs. The
real pod went from 145 seconds to 40 to Ready, with a first 256-token answer in
5.2 ([8](/experiments/llm-cold-start/08-adopt-it-in-production)).

## The caller: 151 seconds to 36.5

<Figure num="Fig. 3" caption="A cold request from send to first token, for each client behaviour and gateway change. Pick one to see what the caller went through.">
  <ColdRequestTimeline set="all" />
</Figure>

Verifying the production change turned up a fine-tuned adapter that loaded 21
seconds after the model, because its loader backed off between health checks.
A fixed two-second check now has it ready first
([9](/experiments/llm-cold-start/09-the-adapter-lagged-the-model)).

Every number so far stopped at the model. Timing a real client through the
gateway showed the model Ready at 41 seconds and a well-behaved client still
waiting until 150.3, because the gateway's `Retry-After: 150` had been sized for
the old start. A client polling every five seconds got its first token at 45.6
([10](/experiments/llm-cold-start/10-measure-what-a-caller-waits)). The gateway
now holds a cold request for up to 60 seconds and forwards it the moment the
model is Ready, so the caller sees one slow answer and no 503: 41.4 seconds
([14](/experiments/llm-cold-start/14-hold-the-cold-request)).

That left Python before the engine as the largest phase. An import profile
attributed 89% of it to imports, paid twice because vLLM spawns its engine
process and the engine imports everything again
([11](/experiments/llm-cold-start/11-attribute-the-22-seconds)). Forking the
process instead fell short of the 20% rule, so adopting it was a judgment call,
and I made it. A cold caller's first token now arrives in 36.5 seconds, median
of three, with no 503s ([15](/experiments/llm-cold-start/15-fork-the-engine-process)).

## What didn't make it in

vLLM's sleep mode freed about 11.5 GiB of the card and served again in two
seconds or less with a warm page cache, but a sleeping model keeps its Kueue quota, and level 1 parks 14 GiB
in host memory on a host that has hung under memory pressure. Level 2 becomes a
third state in deciding how long to keep the model up
([12](/experiments/llm-cold-start/12-sleep-mode-as-a-middle-tier)). The driver
cache setting moved into the shared chart for every vLLM service. The other GPU
services' caches were 112 KB or less, so they were left alone
([13](/experiments/llm-cold-start/13-the-driver-cache-everywhere)).

## What it taught, and what it doesn't show

- **Judge a change by the wait a person feels.** Twice the yardstick moved to
  the caller's clock: when the first request was timed, and when the gateway's
  retry hint hid a faster engine.
- **Keep the hypotheses that fail.** The framework caches changing nothing ruled
  out a layer and pointed at the driver below it.
- **Attribute before optimizing.** The import profile explained 89% of the time
  before the engine and cleared two suspects without a code change.
- **Verify on the real system.** The adapter's lag and a probe that kept waking
  the model were found on production, not on the twin.
- **Small samples on one host.** The numbers describe this card, this vLLM
  version and this machine. GPU-hours returned to experiments aren't measured
  yet, and that needs a week of real traffic.

Still open: choosing the keep-alive window from real traffic, waking only for
inference requests so an uptime check can't keep the model up, and measuring
the 12B's gain from the same driver cache.
