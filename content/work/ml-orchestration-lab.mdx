This lab is where I learn how ML platforms schedule GPU work: queues and quota,
gang scheduling, preemption, and what distributed training does when a node goes
away. It runs on the platform described in [The Homelab](/work/the-homelab), and
it has one GPU, a 16 GB RTX 5060 Ti. The experiment pipeline's design record
states the frame plainly. The lab is for ML-platform patterns that matter at
scale — many GPUs, heterogeneous nodes, fault injection — and it is oversized
for the one card it has today.

The work has two tracks, and they ran months apart. In June, while the lab
briefly had a second GPU, a training series measured data-parallel training
across two mismatched cards: where each step's time went, and what survived
preemption. That second card's host has since died. From late September, a
Kubernetes scheduling stack went in around the remaining card, along with a
pipeline that submits experiments to it. This writeup covers both, and it starts
with the card, because every decision below starts there.

## One card, many claimants

The card is a consumer Blackwell part. It has no MIG partitioning and no vGPU,
so it can't be split in hardware. The cluster's GPU node is an unprivileged LXC
container, which lets the card be shared with the tower's other containers. A VM
would need the card passed through, and passthrough gives the whole card to one
VM.

Inside the cluster, the NVIDIA device plugin time-slices the card into eight
`nvidia.com/gpu` units, and a pod may hold at most one. Time-slicing shares
compute and nothing else. Every slice sees all 16 GB, and a workload that runs
past the card's memory gets an out-of-memory error at run time. The slice count
started at four and went to eight on October 1, because four more always-on
models were each to get a slice of their own. The count is coupled to Kueue's
quota, and the two always change together.

These are the claimants:

- **Resident models**, always on: an embedding server, speech-to-text,
  text-to-speech and object detection.
- **Two on-demand language models**, a 7B and a 12B, scaled to zero by default.
  At most one runs at a time.
- **A GPU notebook**, also scaled to zero until someone needs it.
- **Experiments and training jobs**, submitted as workflow steps or Jobs.
- **GPU CI**, a build agent whose steps run on the card.

The card is bind-mounted into five containers that no scheduler owns. Until late
September, a shell script called the lease stopped and restarted the language
model around heavy jobs. The ruling that replaced it says the lease existed only
because of that topology, and the fix is to remove the topology. The models
moved into the cluster as Deployments, and the scheduler became the arbiter. The
lease has had no tenants since September 30. It is still installed, and
un-mounting the card from the other containers is open work.

### A priority ladder

Every GPU tenant carries a PriorityClass. The ladder, minus one superseded
class, reads:

| Class                     | Value | Who                                       |
| ------------------------- | ----- | ----------------------------------------- |
| `registry-critical`       | 20000 | the in-cluster container registry         |
| `gpu-serving-resident`    | 15000 | the always-on models                      |
| `gpu-training`            | 9000  | training Jobs, the default for GPU steps  |
| `gpu-serving-preemptible` | 5000  | the on-demand language models, notebook   |
| `tower-service`           | 1000  | other services on the GPU node            |
| `gpu-batch`               | 500   | benchmarks, CI, experiments submitted low |

The registry sits at the top on purpose. Tenant images are pulled through it,
and evicting it to admit a GPU pod would strand that pod's own image pull. The
values are sparse so classes can be inserted later. `gpu-batch` also carries
`preemptionPolicy: Never`, which stops kube-scheduler from preempting anything
to place it. That setting governs kube-scheduler only. Kueue compares the
priority value, and a drill showed a training Job evicting a `gpu-batch` Job
through Kueue, with the victim readmitted afterward.

The ladder's ceiling was proven on September 2, before the GPU tiers existed,
with a drill and a negative control staged on memory. A pod in an older burst
class preempted a drill victim at priority −1000. The same pod, aimed at a
`registry-critical` victim, got `Preemption is not helpful for scheduling`.

## Kueue decides whether, Volcano decides where

Two schedulers run here, at different layers, and a design note in the GitOps
repo draws the line between them. Kueue is the admission and quota layer. It
never places a pod on a node. It holds a pod's scheduling gate until the pod's
priority, its queue and that queue's quota say it may proceed, then releases it
to whichever scheduler the pod names. Volcano is a placement layer, and the only
one of the two that understands that N pods must land together.

<Figure num="Fig. 1" caption="A GPU pod's path. In the workflow namespace, Kyverno checks a GPU step came from an approved template. Kueue holds the pod until quota allows. Then kube-scheduler places a single pod, and Volcano places a gang all at once or not at all.">
  <GpuAdmissionFlow />
</Figure>

Gangs need both layers. Kueue's pod integration admits pods one at a time, which
could admit half of a gang that Volcano would then wait on forever. Kueue's
pod-group labels close that gap. When each pod carries a group name and a total
count equal to Volcano's `minAvailable`, Kueue builds no Workload until all N
pods exist, then ungates them together. Volcano then binds all N or none. The
design note's summary is two gang guarantees, one per layer: quota admission in
Kueue and node placement in Volcano. A pod can clear Kueue's quota and still
have nowhere to land, and it can have a node waiting while quota holds it back.
The composition was checked against Kueue's source at the installed version, and
a two-pod gang ran both pods together on the cluster.

One priority ordering has two readers. Volcano's preempt and reclaim actions and
Kueue's preemption compare the same PriorityClass value. Their preemption paths
act on different objects. Volcano evicts pods at placement, and only the slack
above a gang's minimum. Kueue evicts whole Workloads at the quota level,
sometimes before a pod even exists. The case where both act on one gang has not
been exercised.

Volcano's install file records the trade-off of running it. A second scheduler
is more to operate, and two schedulers can race to bind the same resource. The
lighter alternative was the coscheduling plugin. Volcano won on its job
launcher, which hands each pod the peer addresses that Gloo training needs to
rendezvous, and on its fuller set of preemption and fair-share actions.

Kueue went in while the lab has a single GPU node, so that a second node, when
it comes, can be debugged on its own. A portability rule keeps the experiments
out of all of this. Scheduler objects appear only in the platform's GitOps repo.
A study never names a queue, a gang or a GPU resource, and a test in the
experiment repo fails the build if one does. Swapping a scheduler is meant to be
a platform change that touches no study.

## The queues

<Figure num="Fig. 2" caption="The quota shape. Two ClusterQueues share a cohort. The general queue owns all eight slices and lends one. The large-model queue owns none and may borrow one, so at most one language model runs.">
  <GpuQueueTree />
</Figure>

The quota shape is small. Two ClusterQueues share a cohort, so unused quota can
be lent between them. The general queue owns all eight slices and lends one. The
large-model queue owns none and may borrow one. Both language models sit behind
that second queue, which caps them at one running at a time. A nominal quota of
one would have let the cohort admit nine units against eight real ones. Five
LocalQueues feed the two: resident serving, the on-demand models, research,
workflow steps, and CI.

This is where the lease's job went. Its rule was a guard in a systemd unit that
refused to start one model while the other ran. That rule is now a quota.

A drill on September 28, when the card still had four slices, checked it. With
the 7B running, scaling up the 12B left it gated with `insufficient unused quota
for nvidia.com/gpu`, and the card held exactly one large engine. A training Job
at higher priority then reclaimed the borrowed slice. Kueue preempted the 7B
with the reason `InCohortReclamation`, the Job ran on a card with no language
model loaded, and the model was serving again 117 seconds after the Job
finished. That Job ran three one-slice pods. A single one-slice training step
evicting a language model has not been shown.

The queues' resource flavor is pinned to the GPU node by hostname as well as by
label. The dead second GPU node carried the same label, and a workload sent
there could sit Pending in a way that looks exactly like a quota problem.

Slices count shares of compute. Memory is the scarcer resource, and the queues
now carry a VRAM resource too. A small CronJob advertises the card's 15,888 MiB
as an extended resource every ten minutes, and the queues hold quota against it,
with one language model's worth lendable between them. That accounting is inert
today. Every tenant still declares zero, the CI check on declarations only
warns, and nothing caps a process at what it declared. The real guards on memory
are each vLLM server's memory-utilization setting and its startup check for free
memory.

Two alternatives were weighed. Dynamic Resource Allocation with consumable
capacity is the target. It is alpha and off by default in the installed
Kubernetes, Kueue and NVIDIA driver, and the NVIDIA DRA driver can't run beside
the standard device plugin. HAMi was rejected. Its device plugin replaces
NVIDIA's, its scheduler conflicts with Volcano gangs on a single GPU node, and
it needs privileges an unprivileged container can't grant.

## Submitting GPU work

GPU work enters the cluster through Argo Workflows and two approved templates.
The single-GPU step template sets the queue label, the priority, a deadline of
900 seconds by default, memory requests and limits, and a retry policy that
retries on `OnError` only. That retries a pod deleted out from under the step,
as preemption does, and leaves a failure in the study's own code alone. The gang
template creates a Volcano Job whose `minAvailable` matches its replica count,
with Kueue's pod-group labels on each pod.

In the workflow namespace, Kyverno enforces two rules on any pod that requests a
GPU. It must carry a Kueue queue label, and it must carry the annotation the
templates stamp on it. The policy's comment explains the second rule: a
hand-written pod that copies the queue label alone is still rejected. GPU
workloads in the research and serving namespaces carry their queue labels by
convention.

Single-GPU steps are serialized by Argo's own semaphore, set to one. Kueue would
happily admit a second one-slice step while slices are free. The gang template
sits outside that lock on purpose. Review of a benchmark workflow found where
the lock has to sit: a lock on a template releases when that step finishes, so
the benchmark holds its lock for the whole workflow, cleanup included.

Workflows are archived to Postgres for fourteen days, and their logs and
artifacts go to Garage. Finished pods are collected five minutes after a
workflow completes, so log reads aren't raced. The experiment repo's own
database is the durable record of results, so Argo keeps a finished workflow
object for an hour after success and a day after failure. A CI check requires
each of these retention settings to carry a written reason.

## An experiment, end to end

The experiment pipeline lives in its own repository. It began as the harness for
a research program on language-model internals, which is now dormant, and in
late September it was re-scoped as a general lab for running arbitrary
experiments on the cluster.

<Figure num="Fig. 3" caption="An experiment from submit to record. The platform's GPU step template names the queue, so the study never has to. The run row and the telemetry key back to the pod, and the artifacts key to the row.">
  <ExperimentFlow />
</Figure>

**A study** is an importable module with a `main(spec)` function, and a spec is
one JSON object. A sweep is a list of specs.

**The image.** CI publishes one image for the repo from `main`. PyTorch's CUDA
wheels carry the runtime, so there is no CUDA base image. The image is about 4.4
GB compressed, and the first pushes to the registry failed when Traefik's
60-second read timeout cut long blob uploads. Splitting the Python environment
into eight smaller layers didn't help, because buildx uploads the layers in
parallel and each upload still ran past the limit. The platform raised the
timeout, and the image published.

**Submission.** The CLI posts an Argo Workflow straight to the Kubernetes API,
referencing a cell template for one spec or a sweep template for several. It
authenticates as a narrowly scoped service account that can't read Secrets,
can't write Kueue's objects and can't reach the serving namespace, and a wrapper
hands the token to the CLI through a pipe so it never touches disk. The
templates live in the experiment repo, and the GitOps repo syncs them into the
cluster pinned to a commit. The cell template's GPU branch calls the platform's
GPU step template, which is how the experiment repo avoids ever naming a queue.
The spec reaches the container base64-encoded, so a spec can't inject into the
shell command.

**The record.** Each run opens a row in Postgres that records where it ran
(namespace, workflow, pod, retry, image), a manifest of its environment (driver,
CUDA version, GPU, free memory, CPU flags) with its hash, and a hash of its
config. An idempotency key covers the config, a fingerprint of the platform and
toolkit source, the model, the dataset, the condition and the seed, and a
partial unique index allows at most one succeeded row per key. The key has a
known gap: a spec-driven study's own parameters aren't in the config yet, so two
specs that differ only there share a key. Artifacts go to Garage under the
SHA-256 of their bytes. The write order is put, then insert, so the worst a
crash can leave is an orphaned blob, which a garbage-collection command can
delete. Operational telemetry goes to SigNoz and is never read back as data. The
host's per-process GPU memory series carries the pod name, so a run's memory use
joins to its row by pod.

**Endings.** Preemption, a node drain, a retry and a cancel all arrive as
SIGTERM. The run closes its row as `interrupted`, or `cancelled` if a cancel was
requested first, and exits. A retry links back to the attempt it replaces. A
drill on October 3 had a placeholder Job at training priority preempt a
low-priority cell through Kueue. The cell's row closed as `interrupted` 58 ms
after the preemptor's Workload was created. Argo retried the cell, the retry
waited in Kueue for about two and a half minutes, and then it succeeded, linked
to the interrupted attempt. That was one trial. The execution contract records a
race in which Argo would not retry, and one trial can't rule it out. A cancel,
also tested live, closed its row 2.1 seconds after the request, and Argo didn't
retry it.

A pod killed outright can't close its own row, so a reaper runs every ten
minutes and closes rows that have been silent for two hours. Two hours is twice
the one-hour ceiling a GPU step's deadline can be raised to. A false reap costs
more than a slow one: with one success allowed per key, a wrongly closed row
could let a duplicate run start.

**Reading results.** A command joins Argo's node phases to the run rows. Grafana
panels cover failed and stalled runs, with alerts defined and not yet
fire-tested. A small web app shows runs as a spike raster, one row per study and
one tick per run, coloured by outcome.

### The first GPU cell

The first GPU cell submitted to the cluster failed, and no GPU cell could have
succeeded. The cell template's `when` conditions pasted the priority parameter
in unquoted. Every priority class name contains a hyphen, so `gpu-batch` was
parsed as the expression `gpu - batch`. The only check before that submit was a
server-side dry run, which validates the schema and never evaluates a `when`.
Pod-free probes on the live controller reproduced the failure across all eight
combinations of the template's inputs. The fix quoted both sides, and a test now
holds the rule over every workflow file. The quoted form picked the right branch
in all four GPU-and-priority cases, and the resubmitted cell succeeded. The
postmortem's rule is to count a dry run as a schema check and nothing more.

The whole path now works end to end, and so far only a smoke study has used it:
a matrix multiply checked against float64, then a hold on a little GPU memory.
No real study has run on the cluster yet. The sweep template has only been
dry-run, and no CPU cell has run live. A real model study would also hit the
900-second default deadline and the default memory limit. The model-cache mount,
merged on October 5, isn't in the template version the cluster runs yet.

## The Slurm cluster beside it

A small Slurm cluster runs alongside the Kubernetes stack, so that partitions,
QOS, job arrays, GPU GRES and preemption by requeue are learned the Slurm way as
well. It is two unprivileged LXC containers on the GPU host: a controller that
runs the scheduler, the accounting daemon and a local MariaDB and also takes
jobs, and a compute node with the GPU mounted. Terraform builds them, and an
Ansible playbook configures them and re-applies the QOS levels, accounts and
associations idempotently.

Scheduling is backfill over consumable trackable resources. Preemption is by QOS
with requeue, on a ladder of `high`, `normal` and `low`, where high can preempt
both others and normal can preempt low. The batch partition sets
`OverSubscribe=NO`, so a low-priority job holding the cluster has to be
preempted to make room, which is what makes the drill meaningful.

The drill ran in July. A low-QOS two-node job went in with requeue enabled, then
a high-QOS attacker. The attacker completed in 30 seconds, and the victim was
preempted, requeued and restarted from scratch, with one restart recorded. The
cluster has also run a job array, a two-node Gloo all-reduce between the
containers, and a GPU job on the compute node. In September it moved from Slurm
22.05 to 24.11, four releases at once, with accounting recreated, and it gained
the REST API with JWT auth.

The GPU partition is held down. It went down so a Slurm GPU job couldn't collide
with a language model served from another container on the same card. That model
has since moved into the cluster, and the partition is still down. The default
batch partition carries a QOS that refuses GPU requests at submit time. The plan
is Slinky, which runs Slurm on Kubernetes so its GPU jobs fall under Kueue's
quota. It is not built.

## The training series

In June the lab briefly had two GPUs: the 5060 Ti, and an older Quadro P1000
(Pascal, 4 GB) in a laptop. Over two days, a series of phases ran data-parallel
training across them, scheduled by Volcano, and measured it. The two cards need
different PyTorch builds, 2.8 for Blackwell and 2.4 for Pascal, and no NCCL
build spans both, so gradients moved over Gloo, a CPU collective, across the
gigabit LAN. From phase 20 the training loop ran its own all-reduce over Gloo,
flattening gradients to the CPU and weighting each rank by its batch size.

The models are small, 19 million parameters at most, and the numbers below are
measurements on this pair of cards. Their value is in what they show about
mechanisms.

**Preemption.** Volcano preempts only the slack above a gang's `minAvailable`,
so a gang at its minimum can't be preempted at all. A job with a lower minimum
can be, and phase 16 measured the cost for a job that wasn't built for it: 24
synced steps, all lost. Phase 17 added a restart supervisor and a checkpoint
every five steps, and the same event cost four steps. `torchrun` couldn't help
there. Its rendezvous fails across the two PyTorch versions, because 2.8 writes
timezone-aware datetimes and 2.4 compares timezone-naive ones. The phase used
plain `env://` rendezvous with a supervisor, and the job paused while its peer
was gone.

Phase 18 removed the pause. A membership controller of about forty lines, over a
long-lived TCPStore, let the job shrink to one worker and keep training. The
fast card trained alone for about 220 steps during the outage, the slow card
rejoined at live step 271 with the current weights, and the job finished all 600
steps. That refuted an earlier note that this needed etcd or matching PyTorch
versions. The roadmap draws the lesson: protecting a gang and keeping it
preemptible are mutually exclusive, so resilience has to live in the job —
checkpoints, a supervisor, dynamic membership.

**Where the time goes.** Phase 19 split a step into compute, waiting and
network, on a synthetic workload.

<Figure num="Fig. 4" caption="Phase 19, the fast card's step: almost all of it spent waiting for the slow card. The network the series had suspected took 3.4%.">
  <StepTimeSvg />
</Figure>

The working assumption had been a jittery network link. iperf measured 919
Mbit/s with 0.01 ms of jitter and no loss, and the cluster's overlay network
rode the LAN. The cost was the straggler: the P1000 computed about 13 times
longer than the 5060 Ti.

**Fixing the straggler.** Phase 20 moved to a real model, a
3.1-million-parameter character-level GPT with hand-written attention, since the
Pascal build lacks PyTorch's fused attention. Splitting each batch by the cards'
measured speed, 58 and 6 instead of 32 and 32, cut the straggle from 42.6% to
9.5% of the step, a 1.28× speedup. That left the all-reduce at about 82% of the
step. Phase 21 sent gradients in fp16, halving them from 12.8 to 6.4 MB, for
1.41×. Top-k sparsification with error feedback sent the fewest bytes, and its
CPU cost left it behind fp16, though it still beat fp32 at 1.15×. Phase 22
overlapped the all-reduce with the backward pass, using hand-rolled gradient
buckets, in a more compute-bound setting.

<Figure num="Fig. 5" caption="Phase 22, all in one regime. fp16 gradients and overlap each give about 1.2×, and together 1.63×, because the fp16 all-reduce is smaller than the backward pass and overlap hides nearly all of it. Exposed communication fell from 141 ms to 41 ms per step.">
  <OverlapSpeedupSvg />
</Figure>

Phase 21's 1.41× and phase 22's 1.21× for fp16 come from different regimes and
don't combine. Phase 23 measured MFU against model size. Compute MFU rose with
size, from 15.0% to 41.2% on the 5060 Ti and from 14.1% to 51.4% on the P1000,
across 0.11 to 19 million parameters. MFU here is measured against each card's
fp32 CUDA-core peak. It measures utilization, and the P1000 posts the higher
figure while running about ten times slower per token. Phase 23's runs are
logged to MLflow.

The P1000's host died around July. Every two-GPU number above came from hardware
that no longer exists, and the series hasn't been re-run. On one node, the gang
and preemption demos were re-run on October 1, and both passed.

## What the drills found

Most of the stack is a few weeks old. These are the failures that have shaped it
so far, several of them found by drills.

**A fail-closed webhook.** Kueue's webhooks fail closed, which is correct: a GPU
pod that skipped admission would bypass the quota. In late September a memory
incident on the control plane kept Kueue's controller from starting, and pod
creation stopped in every namespace Kueue manages, the drill's own cleanup step
included. The homelab writeup covers the incident. The fix here scoped Kueue's
two pod webhooks to pods that carry a queue label. It is a Kustomize overlay
that renders the same chart and patches an `objectSelector` onto those two
webhooks by name. Two alternatives were rejected. A second Application doing a
server-side-apply patch would be erased by the first Application's next sync,
because Argo CD uses one fixed field manager. A Kyverno policy mutating
admission objects was the other. A drill then created an unlabelled pod while
Kueue was down, and saw a queue-labelled pod refused. Benchmark leftovers now
have their own reaper, a CronJob that Kueue doesn't manage, following the
incident's rule to put the cleanup path outside the failure domain it cleans up
after.

**A real drain.** On October 4 the GPU node was drained for real. An admitted
GPU Job requeued with a new pod and completed, and the resident models were
Ready within a second of uncordon. The drain also took Kueue's controller with
it, since it runs on that node, and its webhook had no endpoints for about eight
minutes.

**Schema acceptance.** One cohort setting was accepted by the CRD's schema and
refused only by Kueue's webhook at sync: borrowing within the cohort requires
reclaiming within it too. It is the same lesson as the first GPU cell's dry run.
A schema says a manifest is well-formed. Only the controller's own webhook says
whether it will take it.

**A dead node.** Turning on time-slicing on September 28 wedged the device
plugin's rollout. The DaemonSet allows one unavailable pod, and that allowance
was held by a pod stuck terminating on the dead P1000 node, which had been
NotReady for 88 days. A forced delete of that pod cleared the wedge. The same
trap was armed in three other DaemonSets, and the dead Node object was deleted
the same day.

**Observability.** GPU telemetry comes from the host. Telegraf reads
`nvidia-smi` every 15 seconds and attributes memory to processes and then to
pods. DCGM was ruled out: profiling support on consumer Blackwell is
unconfirmed, its exporter wants Docker with `SYS_ADMIN`, and DCGM has no
per-process metrics. There are no SM or tensor-core counters. Inside the
cluster, the collector scrapes Argo's and Kueue's controllers and the vLLM
servers' request metrics. Four alerts cover failed workflows, GPU steps pending
in Kueue, control-plane memory and Kueue's availability. Three have fired in
drills, and the pending-GPU alert never has. One drill found that Argo CD's
self-heal undoes a `kubectl scale` used to fake an outage, so the drill used a
zero-pod quota instead.

## What isn't done

The lab has one consumer GPU, a gigabit LAN, small models and no external users.
Within that:

- **Slinky isn't built**, and the Slurm GPU partition is held down.
- **VRAM accounting is wired and inert.** Every tenant declares zero, and DRA,
  the real target, is alpha everywhere it would need to run.
- **A one-slice job preempting a language model is unproven**, and so is the
  case where Kueue and Volcano both preempt within one gang.
- **No real study has run on the cluster.** The sweep template has only been
  dry-run, and checkpoint-and-resume is tested in CI only.
- **The training series ran on hardware that is gone** and hasn't been re-run.
- **GPU CI is a smoke test**, an `nvidia-smi` step. There are no evaluation
  gates.
- **The lease is still installed**, and the card is still mounted into the
  tower's other containers.

## The through-lines

- **The scheduler is the arbiter.** A shell-script lease became a quota, and a
  guard in a systemd unit became a borrowing limit of one.
- **Admission and placement are separate layers.** Kueue decides whether a pod
  may run, Volcano decides where a gang lands, and each gives its own gang
  guarantee.
- **Resilience belongs to the job.** A gang can be protected or preemptible.
  Checkpoints, a supervisor and dynamic membership are what let a job survive
  losing a rank to preemption.
- **A check counts for what it checked.** A dry run checks a schema, a CRD
  checks a shape, and a drill needs a negative control to mean anything.
- **Small numbers, stated as such.** The measurements come from one card, small
  models and two-GPU runs on hardware that is gone. They show how each
  mechanism behaves at that size.
